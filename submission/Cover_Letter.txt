We submit our manuscript, "Quantifying Systematic Bias in Observational Causal Estimates: The Bias Attribution Fraction," for consideration as an Original Investigation in JAMA Network Open.

Observational comparative effectiveness studies increasingly inform clinical guidelines and regulatory decisions, but one question is usually left open: after extensive adjustment, how much of the observed association might still reflect systematic bias rather than a treatment effect? A survey of 237 target trial emulation studies found that only 30.8% addressed unmeasured confounding at all. Negative control calibration can detect residual bias, yet the methods in current use stop at a binary P value and say nothing about how large the bias is relative to the effect that survives calibration.

Our manuscript proposes the bias attribution fraction (BAF), a bounded, continuous diagnostic that extends the OHDSI empirical calibration framework (Schuemie et al.) from a significance test to a quantification of bias. BAF reports the share of the total calibrated signal, bias plus residual effect, that is attributable to systematic bias, on a [0,1] scale, and is computed from quantities that standard calibration workflows with the EmpiricalCalibration R package already produce; no additional data are required. The empirical null used in the case study is fitted on the same cohort, the same covariates, and the same exposure contrast as the target estimates, so the calibration and the estimate it qualifies share one definition. To be explicit about provenance, the calibration framework and the R package are established prior work; the new contributions of this manuscript are the bias attribution fraction itself, the three-zone classification built on it (bias-dominated, mixed, effect-dominated), and their empirical evaluation in a factorial simulation of 1,920,000 repetitions and in a target trial emulation of beta-blocker therapy among 14,677 patients with heart failure from MIMIC-IV.

Adding BAF to the calibrated P value improved discrimination of bias-dominated estimates (AUC, 0.787 to 0.922) and cut the misuse rate from 36.0% to 3.0%. In the case study, the beta-blocker mortality estimate was bias-dominated (BAF = 0.875; 95% credible interval, 0.385 to 0.992), a pattern consistent with systematic healthy-adherer confounding rather than a protective drug effect. A companion question on guideline-directed medical therapy returned a mixed-zone result (BAF = 0.460; 95% credible interval, 0.186 to 0.619), showing how the metric behaves when the calibrated signal neither establishes nor excludes an effect. We emphasize that this methodological demonstration does not constitute evidence that beta-blockers are ineffective for 1-year mortality.

The manuscript runs 2,996 words of text (excluding abstract, references, tables, and figure legends), with 2 tables and 3 figures, within the journal's limits for Original Investigations. The tables and figures follow the references and carry their captions; the baseline characteristics table appears as eTable 15 in Supplement 1. The question the paper addresses, quantifying rather than merely detecting residual confounding, sits close to the quantitative bias analysis framework published in JAMA Network Open last year (Gupta et al. JAMA Netw Open. 2025;8(3):e252152), and we expect readers who found that framework useful will find here a complementary, data-driven counterpart that requires no external evidence.

The case study rests on MIMIC-IV, a public critical care database whose most recent follow-up (2019) predates this submission by more than 5 years. The cohort serves only to demonstrate the metric on real estimates: the contribution is methodological, it does not depend on the currency of the cohort, and all analyses were completed in September 2026.
