We are pleased to submit our manuscript entitled "Quantifying Systematic Bias in Observational Causal Estimates Using Negative Controls: The Bias Fraction" for consideration as an Original Investigation in JAMA Network Open.

Observational comparative effectiveness studies increasingly inform clinical guidelines and regulatory decisions, yet a fundamental question often goes unanswered: after the most sophisticated adjustment methods, how much of the observed association may still reflect systematic bias rather than a true treatment effect? A survey of 237 target trial emulation studies found that only 30.8% addressed unmeasured confounding at all. Negative control calibration can detect residual bias, but existing methods return only a binary p-value, significant or not, without quantifying the magnitude of bias relative to the effect that survives calibration.

Our manuscript proposes the bias fraction (BF), an empirical credibility metric that extends the OHDSI empirical calibration framework from a binary significance test to a bounded, continuous diagnostic. BF reports the share of the total calibrated signal, bias plus residual effect, attributable to systematic bias, on a [0,1] scale. It is computed directly from quantities already produced by standard calibration workflows using the EmpiricalCalibration R package, requiring no additional data. We develop a three-zone heuristic classification (bias-dominated, competitive, effect-dominated), validate it through a factorial simulation across 336 conditions, and demonstrate its application through a target trial emulation of beta-blocker therapy in 14,677 heart failure patients from MIMIC-IV.

The key findings are: (1) BF classification achieved 80.0% accuracy in bias-dominated conditions and 95.1% in effect-dominated conditions, but only 41.8% in the competitive zone, where bias and residual effect are comparable; (2) the beta-blocker mortality estimate was bias-dominated (BF = 0.91; 95% CI, 0.74-0.99), indicating the observed protective association was consistent with systematic healthy-adherer confounding; (3) a known-effect comparison using guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) produced a competitive BF (0.45; 95% CI, 0.31-0.53), illustrating the metric's behavior when a genuine effect survives calibration. We emphasize that this methodological demonstration does not constitute evidence that beta-blockers are ineffective for 1-year mortality.

This manuscript has not been published elsewhere and is not under consideration by another journal. All authors have read and approved the final manuscript and agree to its submission to JAMA Network Open. We have no conflicts of interest to disclose. No external funding was received for this research. The study uses de-identified, publicly available data from MIMIC-IV, which does not require institutional review board approval.

We believe this work is well-suited for JAMA Network Open because it addresses a methodological gap of broad interest to clinicians, guideline panels, and regulatory reviewers who increasingly rely on real-world evidence. The metric is straightforward to compute, requires no additional data collection beyond what standard calibration already provides, and produces an intuitive, bounded summary of estimate credibility that complements existing reporting standards, in the same spirit as the quantitative bias analysis framework recently published in JAMA Network Open (Gupta et al., JAMA Netw Open. 2025;8(3):e252152).
