We are pleased to submit our manuscript entitled "Quantifying Bias in Causal Estimates Using Negative Controls: The Bias Attribution Fraction" for consideration as an Original Investigation in JAMA Network Open.

Observational comparative effectiveness studies increasingly inform clinical guidelines and regulatory decisions, yet a fundamental question often goes unanswered: after the most sophisticated adjustment methods, how much of the observed association may still reflect systematic bias rather than a true treatment effect? A survey of 237 target trial emulation studies found that only 30.8% addressed unmeasured confounding at all. Negative control calibration can detect residual bias, but existing methods return only a binary p-value, significant or not, without quantifying the magnitude of bias relative to the effect that survives calibration.

Our manuscript proposes the bias attribution fraction (BAF), a new empirical credibility metric that extends the pre-existing OHDSI empirical calibration framework (Schuemie et al.) from a binary significance test to a bounded, continuous diagnostic. BAF reports the share of the total calibrated signal, bias plus residual effect, attributable to systematic bias, on a [0,1] scale. It is computed directly from quantities already produced by standard calibration workflows using the EmpiricalCalibration R package, requiring no additional data. To be clear about provenance: the empirical calibration framework and the EmpiricalCalibration R package are established prior work; the new contributions of this manuscript are the BAF and BER metrics, the three-zone classification built on them, and their empirical evaluation. We develop a three-zone heuristic classification (bias-dominated, mixed, effect-dominated), validate it in a factorial simulation of 960,000 repetitions, and demonstrate its application through a target trial emulation of beta-blocker therapy in 14,677 heart failure patients from MIMIC-IV.

The key findings are: (1) adding BAF to the calibrated P value improved discrimination of bias-dominated estimates (AUC, 0.787 to 0.918) and reduced the misuse rate from 36.0% to 2.9%; (2) the beta-blocker mortality estimate was bias-dominated (BAF = 0.91; 95% credible interval, 0.74 to 0.99), indicating the observed protective association was consistent with systematic healthy-adherer confounding; (3) a known-effect comparison using guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) produced a mixed-zone BAF (0.51; 95% credible interval, 0.36 to 0.60), illustrating the metric's behavior when a genuine effect survives calibration. We emphasize that this methodological demonstration does not constitute evidence that beta-blockers are ineffective for 1-year mortality.

We note that the text exceeds the journal's 3,000-word limit for Original Investigations, and that the combined table and figure count is 6 against a limit of 5. The length reflects three components that we believe are each necessary to the claim: the metric definition and interval construction, the 960,000-repetition simulation that calibrates the decision rule against known truth, and the two-question case study that demonstrates the rule on real data. If the editors prefer, we are glad to shorten the text and to move one exhibit (Table 2 or Figure 2) to the Supplementary file; we would welcome the editors' guidance on which reduction they would find most useful. The metric is straightforward to compute, requires no additional data collection beyond what standard calibration already provides, and produces an intuitive, bounded summary of estimate credibility that complements existing reporting standards, in the same spirit as the quantitative bias analysis framework recently published in JAMA Network Open (Gupta et al., JAMA Netw Open. 2025;8(3):e252152).
